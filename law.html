Know your rights

Your federal right to see and get your health information

Federal law gives you the right to see your medical and insurance records, get a copy, and send them to an app or person you choose. This page explains those laws in plain language. Last updated November 23, 2026.

Not legal advice. This is a plain-language summary written for the CollectCare demo. A healthcare attorney should review it before launch. State laws may give you more rights than federal law.

The short version

  • It's your information. Your doctors, hospitals, labs, pharmacies and health plans must let you see and get a copy of your records.
  • You don't need a reason. They can't require you to say why you want it, and they can't refuse because of your reason.
  • They have deadlines. Today they must respond within 30 days, with one 30-day extension. Under a new federal rule, the limit drops to 15 days, with compliance required by July 19, 2027.
  • Electronic copies, your way. If your records are electronic, you can ask for an electronic copy. That includes sending it to a health app you choose.
  • Fees are limited. They can charge only a reasonable, cost-based fee for copies. Under the new rule, viewing your records online or through an app is free.
  • Blocking is against the law. Providers and health IT companies may not block or slow down access to your electronic records without a legal reason.

1. HIPAA: your right of access

45 CFR § 164.524 · Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act Privacy Rule, strengthened by the HITECH Act of 2009

TopicWhat the law says
Who must give access"Covered entities": health plans and most health care providers, such as doctors, hospitals, labs and pharmacies. This includes records a company keeps on their behalf.
Who can askYou, or your "personal representative", such as a parent of a minor or someone with a healthcare power of attorney, within the limits of state law.
What you can getYour "designated record set": medical and billing records, insurance and claims records, lab results, X-rays and other images, and other records used to make decisions about you. It doesn't matter how old the records are, where they're stored, or who created them.
What's not includedPsychotherapy notes kept separate from your chart, and information gathered for a lawsuit or other legal proceeding.
How fastNo later than 30 calendar days. They may take one extension of up to 30 more days, but only if they tell you in writing, within the first 30 days, why it's late and when you'll get it. This shortens to 15 days under the new rule (see section 2).
FormatThey must give you the format you ask for if they can readily produce it. If records are electronic and you want an electronic copy, they must provide one. You can ask for email or mail, and they can't make you come in person to pick it up.
FeesOnly a reasonable, cost-based fee for copying labor, supplies and postage. They can't charge for searching, retrieving or reviewing records, even if state law allows it. They must tell you the approximate fee in advance.
Sending to someone elseYou can direct them in writing to send an electronic copy of records in an electronic health record to a person or company you choose. After a 2020 court decision, this right to direct doesn't cover paper records, and the patient fee limits don't apply to copies sent to third parties.
DenialsAllowed only for narrow reasons. One example is when a licensed professional decides access is reasonably likely to endanger someone's life or physical safety. Being upset by the information is not a valid reason. A denial must be in writing, in plain language, and explain how to request a review and how to complain. Some denials can be reviewed by a different licensed professional.

Sources: HHS Office for Civil Rights, right of access guidance; HHS notice on Ciox Health, LLC v. Azar (2020); 45 CFR § 164.524 (eCFR).

2. New 2026 HIPAA rule: faster, cheaper, app-friendly access

91 Fed. Reg. 74,848 (Nov. 19, 2026) · "HIPAA Privacy Rule: Changes to Support Coordinated Care and Individual Engagement and Reduce Regulatory Burdens"

HHS announced this final rule on November 16, 2026. It takes effect January 19, 2027, and most requirements must be met by July 19, 2027. Until then, the rules in section 1 apply.

  • 15-day deadline. Access "as soon as practicable" and no later than 15 calendar days, down from 30. One extension of up to 30 days is still allowed. If a state law requires faster access, the faster deadline applies.
  • Personal health apps. The right of access expressly includes getting a copy of your electronic records through a personal health app you choose, when the provider or plan can produce them that way. See section 2a for the details.
  • Free in some cases. No fee to inspect your own records or to get electronic records through an internet-based method, such as a patient portal or personal health app.
  • Clearer fees. For electronic copies, only labor may be charged. Covered entities that charge must post a fee schedule.
  • No unreasonable ID hurdles. They can't use identity checks that get in the way of access, such as requiring an in-person visit when remote verification is available.
  • Right to direct, written into the rule. You can direct an electronic copy of records in an electronic health record to a person you designate.

Sources: HHS, 2026 Privacy Final Rule; Federal Register, Nov. 19, 2026 (Doc. 2026-23429).

2a. Sending your records to an app you choose (starting 2027)

45 CFR § 164.501 · § 164.524(c)(2)–(4) · § 164.514(h)(2)(v) · Effective January 19, 2027 · Compliance required by July 19, 2027

The new HIPAA rule spells out, for the first time, your right to pull your records into a "personal health app": an app you use for your own purposes, like CollectCare. Here is what it requires.

TopicWhat the rule says
What counts as your appAn electronic app you use to access your own health information, which can come from many sources. The information must be managed, shared and controlled by you or mainly for you, not by or for a provider or health plan, and not mainly for someone else. A provider's own patient portal or a health plan's member app doesn't count.
When they must send itWhen the records are "readily producible" through your app. That's true if they already share records in that format with others, such as through a secure, standards-based connection (API), and sending them to your app doesn't add security risk to their systems. It's also true if another federal or state law already requires them to offer that kind of connection. If only part of your records can go to the app, they must send that part and give you the rest another way you agree on.
What isn't requiredProviders and plans don't have to buy or build new technology just for apps. They must use what they already have or are already required to have.
Reasons they can't refuseThey can't refuse because the app isn't covered by HIPAA, because they worry the app won't protect your data, because the app doesn't encrypt stored data, or because the app competes with their own service. The only security concern that counts is real risk to their own systems, judged by their HIPAA Security Rule risk analysis.
App registrationThey may ask an app to register before connecting. Registration can't block an app that can connect securely, can't require the app to sign a business associate agreement, and can't push them past the deadline.
It's your requestSending records to your app is treated as you getting your own copy. The app is a tool you use, not a third party. So your full access rights apply, including the stronger fee limits.
FreeNo fee when you get electronic records through an internet-based method, such as a personal health app, a patient portal or an API, with no staff time needed to make the copy.
DeadlineAs soon as practicable, and no later than 15 calendar days, with one 30-day extension only if they explain the delay in writing. The clock starts when they get your request, and verifying your identity or registering the app doesn't stop it.
Identity checksThey can't use unreasonable verification, such as requiring an in-person visit when remote checks work, a "wet" ink signature when an e-signature is valid, or notarization. For requests through national exchange networks, a properly certified digital ID check should be accepted.
After it reaches your appThey may tell you about privacy risks, but they can't refuse because of what might happen after the data leaves their systems. Once your records reach your app, the provider isn't responsible for what the app does with them. The app isn't covered by HIPAA, though other laws, such as the FTC Health Breach Notification Rule, may apply. That's why the app you choose matters.

Health plans, too. Separately, starting January 1, 2027, Medicare Advantage, Medicaid, CHIP and federal Marketplace plans must add prior authorization information to the Patient Access API that apps connect to (see section 4).

Sources: Federal Register, Nov. 19, 2026 (Doc. 2026-23429); HHS, 2026 Privacy Final Rule; CMS-0057-F fact sheet.

3. 21st Century Cures Act: no information blocking

42 U.S.C. § 300jj-52 · 45 CFR Part 171

The Cures Act made sharing electronic health information the expected norm. It bans "information blocking": a practice that is likely to interfere with access to, or exchange or use of, electronic health information, unless a law requires it or a specific exception applies.

Who it applies to
  • Health care providers
  • Developers of certified health IT, such as electronic health record companies
  • Health information exchanges and networks
Limited exceptions
  • Preventing harm to a patient or another person
  • Protecting privacy
  • Protecting security
  • When a request is truly infeasible
  • Keeping systems running, and a few others

"Electronic health information" covers the same records as your HIPAA right of access, when held electronically. The same exclusions apply: psychotherapy notes and information gathered for legal proceedings.

Sources: ASTP/ONC, Information Blocking; 45 CFR Part 171 (eCFR).

4. Health plans must offer a Patient Access API

CMS-9115-F (2020) · CMS-0057-F (2024)

Federal rules require certain health plans to run a secure, standard connection (an "API" built on HL7 FHIR) so you can send your data to an app you choose.

TopicWhat the rules say
Plans coveredMedicare Advantage plans, Medicaid and CHIP (fee-for-service and managed care), and Qualified Health Plans on the federal Marketplace.
What you can getClaims, including what the plan paid and your cost-sharing; encounter data; and clinical data the plan keeps, including lab results, for dates of service from January 1, 2016 onward.
Coming January 1, 2027Information about your prior authorization requests and decisions, except for drugs.
Not coveredEmployer and other plans not listed above don't have to offer this API. You still have your HIPAA right of access to those plans' records.

Sources: CMS, Patient Access API FAQ; CMS Interoperability and Patient Access Final Rule (CMS-9115-F); CMS Interoperability and Prior Authorization Final Rule fact sheet (CMS-0057-F).

If you are refused or delayed

  1. Ask in writing and keep a copy. Note the date: the deadline starts when they receive it.
  2. Ask for the reason in writing. A denial must explain your right to a review.
  3. File a HIPAA complaint with the HHS Office for Civil Rights. Complaints are generally due within 180 days.
  4. Report suspected information blocking through ASTP/ONC.
